    How it assigns the bells

    With the standard number of ringers, you get the standard positions

    Naturals go in pairs: C4 D4, E4 F4, G4 A4, B4 C5 and so on up to B6 C7. Three octaves make eleven positions, as in the Handbell Musicians of America beginner curriculum. Each sharp or flat goes with the natural just below it: C♯ with C, E♭ with D, F♯ with F, A♭ with G, B♭ with A. Some directors give B♭ to the B ringer instead; click the bell to move it.

    With fewer ringers, the light ends join first

    The same curriculum says the bass bells have less rhythmic variety, the middle (battery) is the busy core, C6 to C7 carries the melody, and G6 to C7 rings a little less often. So with fewer ringers the top position goes first, then the bass bells join up, and the middle stays in standard pairs as long as it can.

    Positions stay put from piece to piece

    Usual positions come from your whole set, so Mary is on G4 A4 in every piece. If a piece only uses part of the set, choose "Even out for this piece". Ringers marked 🔒 keep their usual bells either way.

    When someone is out

    • Neighbors cover: the ringers on either side take the missing bells, lower half to the one below, upper half to the one above.
    • High bells to the same note an octave down: a missing treble bell (C6 and up) goes to someone who already rings that note an octave lower, like B6 to the B5 ringer. This is from a Handbell Musicians of America class handout, which says it works best when those ringers can ring four-in-hand. Lower bells still go to the neighbors.

    What it can't see

    It doesn't read your music, so it can't tell when two of one ringer's bells sound together. Look over the hard passages and move a bell if needed. Four bells is a normal load: two in hand, two on the table. More means more picking up and putting down.
